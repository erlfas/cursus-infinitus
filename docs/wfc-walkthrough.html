I
Tiles and sockets

24 modules, four edge types

Every module, whether a street, an aqueduct arch, an insula or a forum stoa, declares what sits on each of its four edges, in the order north, east, south, west. There are only four edge types, called sockets:

Two tiles may sit side by side only when the touching edges carry the same socket. A road edge must meet a road edge, and an aqueduct channel must continue into another aqueduct.

From the socket lists the game precomputes a compatibility table: for every variant and direction, the set of variants allowed next to it. Two "solitary" landmarks, the four-way crossroads and the Capitoline temple, refuse to sit beside a copy of themselves.

II
Superposition

Every cell starts as all 74 possibilities

Before anything is decided, each cell holds the full set of variants, its superposition. The game stores that set as a bitmask. Since 74 bits don't fit in one 64-bit integer, each mask is split into two words of 40 bits, lo and hi. Removing options is just a bitwise AND, so the solver mostly does integer arithmetic.

On the board, an undecided cell shows how many candidates it still has. Darker grey means more uncertainty.

III
Entropy and choosing a cell

Collapse the most constrained cell first

The solver measures uncertainty as weighted Shannon entropy. A cell whose remaining options carry weights w has

Each step picks the uncollapsed cell with the lowest H. This is the minimum-remaining-values heuristic: deal with the cell that has the fewest choices before it runs out of them. A cell with exactly one option left always goes first. A tiny random jitter breaks ties, so equal cells aren't always filled in reading order. On the board, the cell that will be collapsed next has a ring around it.

IV
Collapse

A weighted draw, nudged by the neighbours

The chosen cell is reduced to one variant, drawn at random in proportion to weight. Before the draw, the solver looks at the families of the four neighbours that are already decided and boosts matching candidates. That makes civic and green spaces cluster into forums and parks instead of scattering as single tiles:

Here k is the number of decided neighbours of that family. The random draw comes from a mulberry32 generator seeded with an FNV-1a hash of the seed text, ported bit-for-bit from the original HTML generator. The same seed therefore gives the same chunk.

V
Propagation

One decision ripples outward

Collapsing a cell narrows what its neighbours can be, which narrows their neighbours, and so on. The solver keeps a queue of changed cells. For each one, it takes the union of the compatibility masks of every option the cell still has, per direction, and ANDs that union into the neighbour. If the neighbour's mask shrank, the neighbour joins the queue too.

while head < queue.size():
    var ci = queue[head]; head += 1
    var allowed = union of COMPAT[v][d] for each option v still in cell ci
    for each neighbour ni in direction d:
        var next = mask[ni] & allowed[d]
        if next != mask[ni]:
            mask[ni] = next
            if next == 0: return false     # contradiction
            queue.append(ni)

On the board, cells that propagation just narrowed flash briefly. Early on a single collapse can reach several cells away. Late in the solve most cells are already constrained, and the ripples are short.

VI
Contradiction

When a cell runs out of options, start the chunk again

Sometimes the choices made so far leave a cell with zero candidates. This solver doesn't backtrack step by step. It throws the attempt away and re-solves the chunk with a fresh random stream, seeded seed|cx|cz|attempt, up to 12 times. With these sockets that rarely takes more than one attempt. In the game's headless test, 100 chunks across four seeds needed only one retry in total.

Press Play on a few seeds above. If a contradiction happens, the board marks the empty cell in red, and the next step restarts the chunk.

VII
Going infinite

Chunks that respect their neighbours

A finite grid can be solved in one go. An endless city can't, so the world is split into 8 Ã— 8 chunks. A chunk is solved only when the runner gets close to it. Every chunk is solved with the same steps Iâ€“VI, plus two extra sets of constraints applied before the first collapse.

Seams. For each edge cell of the new chunk, the game checks the neighbour across the chunk border. If that neighbour is already solved, the edge cell is ANDed with what that neighbour allows. The new chunk therefore starts out fitting everything already built around it.

The Roman street grid. Pure WFC could box the runner in with buildings. To stop that, every 16th column is pinned to a northâ€“south road (a cardo), every 16th row to an eastâ€“west road (a decumanus), and every crossing to a four-way junction. A pin doesn't name a single tile. It only requires road sockets along the avenue, so T-junctions, side streets and even aqueduct overpasses can still appear on it. The grid of avenues is endless and connected.

Impossible corners. Rarely, a corner cell touches two older chunks whose edges no single variant can satisfy. Instead of failing the whole chunk, only that cell is relaxed to the variants with the fewest mismatched edges. The rest of the chunk is still solved properly. In the game's headless test it affected at most one tile edge per seed across a 40 Ã— 40-cell area.

VIII
From solver to screen

Solving off the main thread, revealing in collapse order

The streamer keeps the chunks within three chunks of the runner solved, nearest first and biased toward the direction of travel. One chunk is solved at a time on a worker thread, so seam constraints are always taken from finished neighbours.

The reveal is the part you see while running. Each chunk remembers the order the solver collapsed its cells in, and its tiles pop up in that order. The low-entropy, heavily constrained cells near the seams and avenues tend to appear first, and the city fills in behind them, just like the board above. Solved cells stay in memory after their meshes are unloaded, so the streets are unchanged if you run back.
